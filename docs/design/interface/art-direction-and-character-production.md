# Art direction and character production

**ID:** `design.interface.art-direction-and-character-production`
**Status:** `canonical`
**Depends on:** `design.interface.asset-and-language-model-boundary`, `design.representation.identity-presentation-and-persistence`

Presentation uses four image roles. A portrait marks a speaker in dossiers,
rosters, and relationship views. An actor cutout is a transparent waist- or
thigh-up character reused across conversations, meetings, offices, and
hearings. A background plate is a reusable institutional location. An event
illustration is a bespoke composition for a sufficiently important historical
or dramatic moment. Other visual systems, such as props, rigs, shot schemas,
wardrobe schemas, or procedural blocking, are added only after the same
production failure recurs.

Characters read as animals first. The head is built from the animal's own
skull, face planes, eyes, muzzle or beak, fur or feathers, and silhouette.
Human skin, scalp, ears, noses, facial planes, and literal hairstyles never
sit on an animal head; historical hair becomes species-native fur, plumage,
bristles, markings, or grooming. Identity comes from species-native anatomy
plus a few cues such as glasses, age, posture, gaze, clothing, grooming, and
silhouette. Bodies stay broadly humanoid where period clothing and interaction
require it. Gender presentation comes from period clothing, grooming,
silhouette, posture, and styling, never from exaggerated eyelashes, lipstick,
or similar shortcuts.

Production effort follows recurrence and salience. A figure that recurs across
portraits, cutouts, meetings, and event art, where drift would be visible,
receives a bespoke character bible. Supporting historical figures use a species
guide, age and build, three to five identity cues, and wardrobe. Background
actors use generic species and body treatment. A species guide covers head and
body proportions, the neck-to-clothing transition, fur or feather treatment,
hands, clothed silhouette, and generation failures observed in use.

Generated images are replaceable outputs. The reviewed bible, guide, and
approved asset metadata define a character; a PNG never does. Species,
costume, and caricature remain presentation data under the identity rule and
carry no behavior, authority, competence, protected identity, or moral worth.

The period direction for late-1970s and 1980s scenarios is gritty
institutional pixel art: low-resolution hand-pixeled clusters, restrained
dithering, angular shadows, a muted palette of olive, tobacco brown, dirty
beige, charcoal, and desaturated rust, worn backgrounds, and flat overhead
light. Characters are serious and unsmiling rather than mascots. Source images
contain no gradients, gloss, text, logos, flags, interface, watermark, or
signature. This direction is validated for portraits only; cutouts, plates,
and event illustrations require their own evidence before approval.
