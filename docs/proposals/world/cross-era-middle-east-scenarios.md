# Cross-era Middle East scenario-family proposal

**ID:** `proposal.world.cross-era-middle-east-scenarios`
**Status:** `proposal`
**Depends on:** `proposal.world.national-resource-management`, `proposal.world.arabian-reference-chain`
**Proposes changes to:** `design.campaign.scenario-and-campaign-composition`, `design.economy.opening-conditions-and-transmission`, `design.interface.learning-and-run-knowledge`

This proposal is nonbinding. It makes no catalog entry or scenario ready.

The Middle East would anchor a cross-era scenario family asking one comparative
question: given different political formulations of the region, how do
materially similar resource dependencies produce different economic realities
and Federal Reserve decision problems? The family would reuse stage contracts
and product vocabulary while preserving period-specific actors, law,
infrastructure, accounts, market structure, information, and policy authority.

Three initial lenses would exercise different causal regimes:

- **Paul Vulture, 1979–1981:** the Iranian Revolution and Iran–Iraq conflict
  reduce and destabilize supply inside an economy with entrenched inflation,
  adaptive wage and price behavior, weak policy credibility, and speculative
  inventory demand. The monetary problem is persistence and de-anchoring, not
  merely the contemporaneous oil quantity.
- **Alan Greenspaniel, 1990–1991:** Iraq's invasion of Kuwait removes production
  while coalition formation, replacement output, strategic stocks, and the
  expected duration of war shape the path back. The monetary problem combines
  inflation risk with recession, confidence, credit weakness, and uncertainty
  over whether the price shock will reverse.
- **Kevin Boarsh, contemporary Iran/Hormuz:** attacks, navigation restrictions,
  sanctions, insurance, carrier and crew refusal, alternative routes, storage
  saturation, and producer shut-ins separate recoverable resources from
  deliverable supply. The monetary problem crosses inflation credibility,
  freight, global dollar funding, foreign portfolios, and financial stress.

The common comparison spine would be:

`political configuration -> authority and commitments -> production and`
`allocation -> physical execution -> exportability -> delivered supply ->`
`prices and cash flows -> beliefs and balance sheets -> Fed-facing evidence`

No scenario would encode “Middle East tension” as a scalar modifier. Political
state would matter only through attributable decisions, effective legal or
contractual changes, security conditions, infrastructure constraints,
counterparty responses, and observations. A declared embargo, invasion,
escort, output pledge, corridor, sanction, or ceasefire would still have to
pass through its owners and downstream stages.

Political formulations would be sibling-fixture inputs, not products of a
regional politics engine. Each historical lens could vary one consequential
formulation while holding the initial domestic macro regime fixed:
producer coordination versus defection; narrow route restriction versus broad
closure; credible short duration versus open-ended conflict; successful versus
failed replacement supply; or shared versus fragmented Gulf security. The
fixtures would not force equal-sized shocks. They would test whether players
identify why apparently similar price moves imply different persistence,
distribution, financial transmission, and appropriate Fed responses.

Runtime political variation would follow
`doc:design.economy.external-provider-contracts`; the proposal adds no broader
foreign-politics simulation.

Each scenario would classify historical content as immutable opening prefix,
anchored exigency, conditional exigency, or excluded development. Anchors would
preserve a causal challenge rather than require its literal historical costume;
conditional events would declare predicates, timing, variation, and expiry.
Random incidents and player inputs could alter history inside that vocabulary.
If a plausible structural divergence escaped it, the run could conclude or
continue through a declared fallback while exporting a divergence dossier with
the seed, causal trace, material state, and missing content boundary.

Cross-era evaluation would compare causal explanation and transferable judgment,
not raw outcome levels. Period research would determine historical baselines,
available evidence, authorities, reaction functions, and calibration bands;
counterfactual branches would remain plausible departures rather than rewritten
history.
