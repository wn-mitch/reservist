# Gulf logistics and chokepoints proposal

**ID:** `proposal.world.logistics-chokepoints`
**Status:** `proposal`
**Depends on:** `proposal.world.petroleum-gas-system`, `proposal.world.ownership-and-interfaces`
**Proposes changes to:** `design.economy.real-economy-network`, `design.economy.opening-conditions-and-transmission`

This proposal is nonbinding. It makes no catalog entry or scenario ready.

Exportability would be a staged service, not a multiplier on production. A
shipment would require sellable inventory, storage headroom, a nomination,
pipeline or berth admission, a compatible terminal, a vessel, crew acceptance,
insurance and finance eligibility, security clearance, passage capacity,
destination acceptance, unloading capacity, and settlement. Failure at any
stage would leave an attributable queue, cost, delay, diversion, or default.

The minimum promoted network would distinguish:

- Persian Gulf loading terminals and feeder pipelines;
- the Strait of Hormuz passage service;
- the Saudi East-West pipeline and Yanbu/Red Sea outlet;
- the Abu Dhabi crude pipeline and Fujairah/Gulf of Oman outlet;
- Omani terminals outside Hormuz;
- Iraqi southern terminals and their narrow gathering approaches;
- Bab el-Mandeb, Red Sea, Suez/SUMED, and Cape diversion providers when used;
- crude tankers, product tankers, and LNG carriers as incompatible fleets; and
- destination ports, inventories, and refinery or regasification acceptance.

A route mechanism would own capacity, transit time, queue, damage, closure or
restriction state, operating rules, and witnessed passage. Its owner could
allocate or ration access only where period law grants that authority. A
security provider could change attack risk, inspection, convoy timing, or
permitted zones; it could not directly erase a cargo, tanker, or contract.
Carriers, crews, insurers, banks, charterers, terminal operators, and buyers
would each make bounded acceptance decisions from their own information and
eligibility rules.

Storage would close the upstream loop. Producers would own titled inventories;
storage operators would own tank capacity, custody, injection and withdrawal
queues, compatibility, and loss. When loading falls below extraction, storage
fills by grade and location. The producer must then redirect, discount, reduce
output, or shut in. Restart time and field damage would be explicit where
material. Strategic stocks at importing countries would remain separate owners
whose releases alter delivered availability, not Gulf production.

Alternative routes would have dated, product-specific usable capacity rather
than binary bypass flags. They would consume feeder capacity and may move only
some origins or grades. The composition would preserve the difference between
physical closure, attack probability, navigation rule, insurance exclusion,
crew refusal, and buyer avoidance even when all reduce observed traffic.

Calibration would use period-specific operator data and the
[EIA oil chokepoints analysis](https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/special-topics/world_oil_transit_Chokepoints).
The contemporary baseline would separately reconcile Hormuz crude, product, and
LNG flows with bypass capacity. Historical profiles would use infrastructure
that actually existed at the time rather than back-porting today's optionality.
